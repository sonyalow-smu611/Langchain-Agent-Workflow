Photosynthesis: Chapter 4 Notes

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. The overall equation is 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6O2. It takes place in the chloroplasts, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll.

The light-dependent reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes. Light is absorbed by chlorophyll in photosystem II and photosystem I. Water is split in a process called photolysis, releasing oxygen as a by-product, hydrogen ions and electrons. The electron transport chain produces ATP by chemiosmosis through ATP synthase, and NADP is reduced to NADPH.

The light-independent reactions, also called the Calvin cycle, happen in the stroma. The enzyme RuBisCO fixes carbon dioxide onto ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP), a 5-carbon compound. The resulting 6-carbon compound splits into two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP). ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent reactions reduce GP to triose phosphate (TP). Some TP is used to make glucose, and the rest regenerates RuBP.

Limiting factors of photosynthesis include light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. At low light intensity, the rate increases as light increases. Temperature affects enzymes such as RuBisCO; above about 45 degrees Celsius enzymes denature and the rate falls sharply. Farmers use greenhouses to control these limiting factors and increase crop yield.

Key dates: Jan Ingenhousz showed in 1779 that light is needed for plants to produce oxygen. Melvin Calvin mapped the Calvin cycle in the 1950s and won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1961.
